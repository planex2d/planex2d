The coastline paradox

How long is the coast of Great Britain? Lewis Fry Richardson measured coasts with dividers: set them to a length r, walk them along the coast from point to point, and count the steps. The shorter the ruler, the more of the coast’s bays and headlands it follows, and the longer the coast becomes, without a limit in sight. For many coasts, the length grows as a power of the ruler, L ∝ r^(1 − D). Mandelbrot called D the fractal dimension of the coast and quoted D ≈ 1.25 from Richardson’s measurements of Britain’s west coast. A smooth curve has D = 1.

Here, the dividers are first checked on the Koch snowflake, whose dimension is known exactly, and then walked along the coast of Great Britain.

[1]:

import math

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np
import planex as px

The dividers

dividers walks along a closed polyline. From the current point, the next point is where the line first leaves the circle of radius r around it, and the walk steps there. When less than r is left, the remaining distance to the start is added. A point that lies within 10⁻⁹ r of the circle counts as on it, since the vertices of the Koch snowflake lie exactly on the circles.

[2]:

def dividers(points, r):
    line = np.vstack([points, points[:1]])
    here, i, walk = line[0], 0, [line[0]]
    while True:
        far = np.nonzero(np.hypot(*(line[i + 1:] - here).T) >= r * (1 - 1e-9))[0]
        if len(far) == 0:
            return (len(walk) - 1) * r + math.dist(line[-1], here), np.array(walk)
        i += far[0]
        a, b = line[i], line[i + 1]
        u, f = b - a, a - here
        p, q = u @ f / (u @ u), (f @ f - r**2) / (u @ u)
        here = a + (-p + math.sqrt(max(p * p - q, 0))) * u
        walk.append(here)

A check: the Koch snowflake

The Koch snowflake starts from an equilateral triangle and puts a bump on the middle third of every edge, again and again. Each round multiplies the perimeter by 4/3, so dividers of length 3^(−m) measure 3 · (4/3)^m, and D = log 4 / log 3, about 1.2619. The example builds six rounds, 12288 edges, as a planex2d face. Its perimeter and area agree with the series of the construction:

[3]:

def koch(a, b, rounds):
    if rounds == 0:
        return [a]
    third = (b - a) / 3
    turn = np.array([[0.5, math.sqrt(3) / 2], [-math.sqrt(3) / 2, 0.5]])
    c, e = a + third, a + 2 * third
    d = c + turn @ third
    return [p for s, t in ((a, c), (c, d), (d, e), (e, b)) for p in koch(s, t, rounds - 1)]


corners = [np.array((0.0, 0.0)), np.array((1.0, 0.0)), np.array((0.5, math.sqrt(3) / 2))]
rounds = 6
points = np.array([p for k in range(3) for p in koch(corners[k], corners[(k + 1) % 3], rounds)])
flake = px.Face([px.FromPolyline(points, closed=True)])
triangle = math.sqrt(3) / 4
area = triangle * (1 + sum(3 * 4 ** (n - 1) / 9**n for n in range(1, rounds + 1)))
print(len(flake.edges), "edges, perimeter", flake.outer.mass, "exact", 3 * (4 / 3) ** rounds)
print("area", flake.mass, "exact", area)

12288 edges, perimeter 16.855967078186733 exact 16.855967078189295
area 0.6908178956345367 exact 0.6908178956345377

The dividers give the exact lengths, and the slope of log L over log r gives D:

[4]:

rulers = [3.0**-m for m in range(1, 6)]
lengths = [dividers(points, r)[0] for r in rulers]
print("lengths", [round(L, 12) for L in lengths])
print("exact  ", [round(3 * (4 / 3) ** m, 12) for m in range(1, 6)])
slope = np.polyfit(np.log(rulers), np.log(lengths), 1)[0]
print("D", 1 - slope, "exact", math.log(4) / math.log(3))

The coast of Great Britain

The coast is Great Britain’s outline from the 1:10m coastline of Natural Earth, which is in the public domain. It is projected to kilometers around the middle of the island and stored as britain-coast.csv next to this page: 3706 points, about 2 km apart.

[5]:

coast = np.loadtxt("britain-coast.csv", delimiter=",", comments="#")
island = px.Face([px.FromPolyline(coast, closed=True)])
print(len(coast), "points, coast", island.outer.mass, "km")

3706 points, coast 7319.654743722943 km

Walked with dividers from 5 to 160 km, the coast grows from about 2600 km to more than 6000 km. In a log-log plot, the lengths lie close to a line, and its slope gives D near Mandelbrot’s 1.25:

[6]:

rulers = [5, 10, 20, 40, 80, 160]
walks = {r: dividers(coast, r) for r in rulers}
lengths = [walks[r][0] for r in rulers]
for r, L in zip(rulers, lengths):
    print("ruler", r, "km: coast", round(L), "km")
slope, offset = np.polyfit(np.log(rulers), np.log(lengths), 1)
print("D", 1 - slope)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 3.6))
ax.loglog(rulers, lengths, "o", label="dividers")
ax.loglog(rulers, np.exp(offset) * np.array(rulers, dtype=float) ** slope, "--", label=f"D = {1 - slope:.2f}")
ax.minorticks_off()
ax.set_xticks(rulers, [str(r) for r in rulers])
ax.set_yticks([2500, 4000, 6000], ["2500", "4000", "6000"])
ax.set_xlabel("ruler in km")
ax.set_ylabel("coast in km")
ax.legend();

Below a few kilometers, the data itself sets the limit: with points about 2 km apart, the polyline is the coast at that resolution, and its own length, 7320 km, is what the shortest dividers approach. A finer map would give a longer coast again.
